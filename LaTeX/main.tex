\begin{abstract} \noindent
Annuity values depend on the shape of mortality at old ages. In the gamma-Gompertz model, known to actuaries as Beard's law, the population hazard always levels off at old ages when the frailty variance is positive. We study the gamma--Laguerre--Weibull (GLW) model, which adds gamma frailty to the Laguerre--Weibull (LW) distribution and contains the gamma-Gompertz model as a special case. The LW distribution, a known Weibull extension, is derived from the generating function of the Laguerre polynomials, which gives a series representation in terms of distributions of maxima. The model includes a shape parameter $b$ that, for positive frailty variance, determines whether the population hazard grows without bound, levels off or declines at old ages. We derive moments, generating functions, entropy and Kullback--Leibler divergence, and life annuities, assurances and joint-life statuses. Parameters are estimated by maximum likelihood, and $b=1$ is tested by a likelihood-ratio test, bootstrap-calibrated when the frailty variance is estimated at zero. Simulations show small bias and correct test size. For female mortality at ages 50--109 in six countries, 1950--2020, $\hat b$ rises from about 1 to 1.23--1.37, the gamma-Gompertz restriction is rejected in every year from 1990, and the GLW model has the lowest AIC in 78\% of population-years among four models.\\
[0.5em]
\textbf{Keywords:} Laguerre polynomials; gamma frailty; gamma-Gompertz model; old-age mortality; life annuities; Poisson likelihood; likelihood-ratio test; Human Mortality Database.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%  ------------------------------------------------------------

The value of a life annuity or a pension depends on survival at ages where data are sparse. An annuity bought at 65 pays for as long as the annuitant lives, so its price depends on how fast the force of mortality rises after 90, and insurers need reliable estimates of mortality at the oldest ages to price life annuities and reverse mortgages \citep{gbari2017extreme}. Parametric mortality laws are the standard tool for this purpose. A law with few parameters graduates the observed rates, extends them beyond the data, and, when its survival function is available in closed form, gives premiums, reserves and annuity values as functions of the parameters. The law of \citet{gompertz1825}, in which the force of mortality grows exponentially with age, and its extensions by Makeham, Perks and Beard are the classical examples used in insurance and pensions practice \citep[Section~5.9]{macdonald2018modelling}. \citet{castellares2022gompertz, castellares2024gamma} derived closed-form annuity and assurance values for the Gompertz--Makeham and gamma-Gompertz--Makeham models.

What a law implies at the oldest ages is largely fixed by its functional form. In human populations, the increase of mortality slows down at old ages and the hazard falls below the Gompertz curve \citep{vaupel1998biodemographic}. Unobserved heterogeneity explains part of this pattern: the frailest individuals die first, so the population hazard rises more slowly than the hazard of any individual \citep{vaupel1979impact}. If the frailty $Z$ multiplies an individual hazard $\mu$ and follows a gamma distribution with mean one and variance $\sigma^2$, the population hazard is $\mu(x)/\{1+\sigma^2\int_0^x\mu(t)\,\dd t\}$, and the survivors to age $x$ again have gamma-distributed frailty, with a mean that decreases with age \citep{vaupel1979impact}. With the Gompertz hazard $\mu(x)=\theta a\,\e^{ax}$, this gives the gamma-Gompertz model, known in actuarial work as Beard's law \citep[Table~5.1]{macdonald2018modelling}. For $\sigma^2>0$, its hazard always levels off at $a/\sigma^2$. The plateau is therefore a property of the model and not a conclusion drawn from the data. Whether old-age mortality levels off is disputed. \citet{gbari2017extreme} found no levelling off in Belgian extinct cohorts above age 95, and noted that assuming a constant force of mortality at the oldest ages is a conservative strategy for managing life annuities. A model for annuity work should allow the old-age hazard to keep rising, level off or decline, and let the data decide.

We propose such a model. The individual hazard is $\theta ab\,x^{b-1}\e^{ax^b}$, which is the Gompertz hazard when $b=1$. The corresponding distribution is a known Weibull extension \citep{xie2002modified}, which generalises the model of \citet{chen2000new} and was studied further by \citet{tang2003statistical}. We derive it from the generating function of the Laguerre polynomials and call it the Laguerre--Weibull (LW) distribution. Adding gamma frailty gives the gamma--Laguerre--Weibull (GLW) model, which contains the gamma-Gompertz model as the case $b=1$ and the LW distribution as the case $\sigma^2=0$. For $\sigma^2>0$, the GLW hazard behaves like $ab\,x^{b-1}/\sigma^2$ at old ages, so a single parameter decides whether it grows without bound ($b>1$), levels off ($b=1$) or declines ($b<1$), and the plateau of the gamma-Gompertz model becomes a hypothesis that can be tested.

The Laguerre construction starts from the factor $\exp\{-\theta t/(1-t)\}$ of the generating function. Read as a survival function on $(0,1)$, it defines a one-parameter distribution, which we call the Laguerre distribution. Composing it with a baseline distribution function $G$ gives the survival function $\exp\{-\theta G/(1-G)\}$, an exponential distribution applied to the odds $G/(1-G)$, whose hazard is the baseline hazard multiplied by the increasing factor $\theta/(1-G)$. This family belongs to the T-X class of \citet{alzaatreh2013new} and is the Weibull-G family of \citet{bourguignon2014weibull} with the Weibull shape fixed at one. The Laguerre polynomials give its distribution function as a series in $G^k$, the distribution functions of maxima of $k$ independent copies from $G$, with coefficients that are differences of consecutive Laguerre polynomials (Proposition~\ref{prop:series}). The LW distribution is the case of a Weibull baseline.

For the analysis, one representation does most of the work. If $X$ follows the GLW model, then $\sigma^2\theta\{\exp(aX^b)-1\}$ has a Pareto type II distribution, and $aX^b$ follows the gamma-Gompertz model with unit rate. This gives the moments and the generating functions in terms of special functions, and also the Shannon entropy and the Kullback--Leibler divergence, which we give in Appendix~\ref{app:dist}. For the actuarial calculations, if $T_x$ is the future lifetime at age $x$, then $a(x+T_x)^b-ax^b$ has the distribution of $aX^b$ with $\theta$ replaced by $\theta_x$, which is $\theta\e^{ax^b}$ times the mean frailty of the survivors to age $x$. The effect of selection on the survivors is thus summarised by a single parameter. When $b=1$ the future lifetime at every age is again gamma-Gompertz, so the closed forms of \citet{castellares2024gamma} apply, and under a restriction on the parameters the joint-life status of two lives of the same age is again a GLW model. In general, life annuities and assurances are one-dimensional integrals of an explicit survival function.

The parameters are estimated by maximum likelihood from death counts and central exposures to risk, with the death counts modelled as Poisson variables \citep{brillinger1986biometrics, macdonald2018modelling}, and the gamma-Gompertz model is tested against the GLW model with a likelihood-ratio test. We assess both in a simulation study. We then fit the model to female mortality after age 50 in six countries from 1950 to 2020, using the Human Mortality Database \citep{hmd}, compare it with the Gompertz, Kannisto and gamma-Gompertz laws, and compute remaining life expectancies, annuities and assurances at ages 50, 65 and 80.

The contribution of the paper is the mortality model built on the LW distribution, together with the Laguerre representation, the analytical and actuarial properties of the model, likelihood inference with a test of the gamma-Gompertz restriction, and the application to seventy years of mortality data. Section~\ref{sec:laguerre} introduces the Laguerre distribution. Section~\ref{sec:family} defines the Laguerre-G family and the LW distribution. Section~\ref{sec:glw} presents the GLW model, and Section~\ref{sec:actuarial} its actuarial properties. Section~\ref{sec:inference} treats statistical inference, Section~\ref{sec:simulation} reports a simulation study, Section~\ref{sec:application} presents the application, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes. Appendix~\ref{app:dist} contains further distributional results, and Appendix~\ref{app:application} additional results of the application.

\section{The Laguerre distribution}\label{sec:laguerre}
%  ------------------------------------------------------------

The generating function of the Laguerre polynomials, given in Equation~\eqref{exp} below, contains the factor $\exp\{-\theta y/(1-y)\}$. A function $\exp\{-\theta\,k(y)\}$ with $k$ increasing from 0 to infinity is a survival function with cumulative hazard $\theta k$, and it has the exponential form of the Gibbs weights of statistical mechanics \citep[see][]{georgii2011gibbs}. With $k(y)=y/(1-y)$ on $(0,1)$, this survival function can be expanded in terms of Laguerre polynomials, as in Equation~\eqref{family}. The expansion gives the representations of this section and of Section~\ref{sec:family}, where the survival function is composed with a baseline distribution, which multiplies the baseline hazard by a factor that increases with age. Throughout, $Y$ denotes a random variable on $(0,1)$ with the Laguerre distribution, $X$ a lifetime, and $\Exp$ a standard exponential random variable.

\subsection{Laguerre polynomials}

The Laguerre polynomials are
\begin{equation}\label{def-Lag}
L_n(\theta) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k}\, \frac{(-1)^k\,\theta^k}{k!}, \qquad n\in\N=\{0,1,2,\dots\}.
\end{equation}
More generally, the associated Laguerre polynomials $L_n^{(\alpha)}$, $\alpha>-1$, have generating function
\begin{equation}\label{exp}
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} L_n^{(\alpha)}(\theta)\, y^n = \frac{1}{(1-y)^{\alpha+1}}\,\exp\left\{-\frac{\theta y}{1-y}\right\}, \qquad |y|<1,
\end{equation}
with $L_n=L_n^{(0)}$ \citep[see][]{lebedev1965special, rainville1960special}. For $\theta\geq 0$, $|L_n(\theta)|\leq \e^{\theta/2}$ for all $n$ \citep{szeg1939orthogonal}. Setting $\alpha=0$ in Equation~\eqref{exp} gives
\begin{equation}\label{family}
\exp\left\{-\frac{\theta y}{1-y}\right\}=(1-y)\sum^{\infty}_{n=0} L_{n}(\theta) \, y^n, \qquad |y|<1.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Definition and properties}

For $\theta>0$, the function $\phi(y)=\exp\{-\theta y/(1-y)\}$ is continuous and strictly decreasing on $(0,1)$, and it goes from 1 at $y=0$ to 0 as $y\to1$. It is therefore a survival function on $(0,1)$.

\begin{definition}
A random variable $Y$ has the Laguerre distribution with parameter $\theta>0$, written $Y\sim\Lag(\theta)$, if its distribution function is
\[
H(y)=1-\exp\left\{-\frac{\theta y}{1-y}\right\}, \qquad 0<y<1.
\]
\end{definition}

The Laguerre distribution is not new as a distribution on $(0,1)$. It is the Weibull-uniform distribution of \citet[Section~3.1]{bourguignon2014weibull}, also known as the Phani distribution, with Weibull shape one and uniform baseline on $(0,1)$, and the unit exponential distribution of \citet{bakouch2023unit}, $1-\exp[\alpha\{1-((1+y)/(1-y))^\beta\}]$, with $\beta=1$ and $\alpha=\theta/2$. What is new here is its connection with the generating function of the Laguerre polynomials, which gives the series representations below. Since $Y/(1-Y)$ is exponentially distributed (Proposition~\ref{prop:basic}(i)), maximum likelihood estimation of $\theta$ from a sample of $Y$ reduces to estimation of an exponential rate; \citet{bakouch2023unit} treat estimation in their two-parameter family.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:basic}
Let $Y\sim\Lag(\theta)$.
\begin{enumerate}\renewcommand{\labelenumi}{(\roman{enumi})}
\item $Y/(1-Y)=\Exp/\theta$, where $\Exp$ is a standard exponential random variable; equivalently, $Y=\Exp/(\theta+\Exp)$.
\item The quantile function is $H^{-1}(u)=-\log(1-u)/\{\theta-\log(1-u)\}$, $0<u<1$.
\item The density and the hazard function are
\[
h(y)= \frac{\theta}{(1-y)^2}\, \exp\left\{-\frac{\theta y}{1-y}\right\},
\qquad
\nu(y)= \frac{\theta}{(1-y)^2}, \qquad 0<y<1.
\]
The hazard increases from $\theta$ to infinity.
\item For $0<y<1$,
\[
H(y)=y-\sum^{\infty}_{k=1} L_{k}(\theta) \, (y^k-y^{k+1}),
\qquad
h(y)=\theta\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} L_k^{(1)}(\theta)\, y^k.
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) For $t>0$, the event $\{Y/(1-Y)>t\}$ is the event $\{Y>t/(1+t)\}$. Hence $\Pr\{Y/(1-Y)>t\}=1-H\{t/(1+t)\}=\e^{-\theta t}$, which is the survival function of $\Exp/\theta$.

(ii) Setting $H(y)=u$ gives $\theta y/(1-y)=-\log(1-u)$, and solving for $y$ gives $H^{-1}(u)$.

(iii) Differentiating $H$ gives $h(y)=\theta(1-y)^{-2}\phi(y)$, because $\dd\{y/(1-y)\}/\dd y=(1-y)^{-2}$. The hazard is $\nu(y)=h(y)/\{1-H(y)\}=h(y)/\phi(y)=\theta(1-y)^{-2}$, which increases from $\theta$ at $y=0$ to infinity as $y\to1$.

(iv) By Equation~\eqref{family}, $1-H(y)=(1-y)\sum_{k\geq0}L_k(\theta)y^k=\sum_{k\geq0}L_k(\theta)(y^k-y^{k+1})$. Since $L_0=1$, the term with $k=0$ is $1-y$, and the expansion of $H$ follows. For $h$, Equation~\eqref{exp} with $\alpha=1$ gives $(1-y)^{-2}\phi(y)=\sum_{k\geq0}L_k^{(1)}(\theta)y^k$; multiplying by $\theta$ gives $h(y)$ by part (iii).
\end{proof}

Parts (ii) and (iii) give the quantile and hazard functions of the Laguerre-G family in Section~\ref{sec:family}, and part (iv) its series representation. Part (i) is the key to the stochastic representation of the LW and GLW models in Section~\ref{sec:glw}. The moments of $Y$ have closed forms in terms of the exponential integral, and they lead to a series for the exponential integral in terms of Laguerre polynomials; both are given in Appendix~\ref{app:laguerre-moments}.

\section{The Laguerre-G family and the Laguerre--Weibull distribution}\label{sec:family}
%  ------------------------------------------------------------

To obtain lifetime distributions, we transform the Laguerre distribution through a baseline distribution function.

\subsection{Definition}

Let $Y\sim\Lag(\theta)$, and let $G$ be a continuous distribution function on $(0,\infty)$ with density $g$, hazard $\mu_G=g/(1-G)$ and quantile function $G^{-1}$. We define the Laguerre-G random variable $X=G^{-1}(Y)$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:family}
The distribution function, the hazard function and the quantile function of $X$ are
\[
F(x)=1- \exp\left\{-\theta\,\frac{G(x)}{1-G(x)}\right\},
\qquad
\mu_F(x)=\frac{\theta}{1-G(x)}\,\mu_G(x),
\]
and
\[
F^{-1}(u)=G^{-1}\left\{\frac{-\log(1-u)}{\theta-\log(1-u)}\right\}, \qquad 0<u<1.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $G$ is continuous and increasing, $\{X\leq x\}=\{Y\leq G(x)\}$, so $F(x)=H\{G(x)\}$, which gives the expression for $F$, and $F^{-1}=G^{-1}\circ H^{-1}$, which gives $F^{-1}$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:basic}(ii). Differentiating $F(x)=H\{G(x)\}$ gives the density $f(x)=g(x)\,h\{G(x)\}$, and dividing by $1-F(x)=\phi\{G(x)\}$ gives the hazard $\mu_F(x)=g(x)\,\nu\{G(x)\}=\theta g(x)/\{1-G(x)\}^2$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:basic}(iii). This equals $\theta\mu_G(x)/\{1-G(x)\}$.
\end{proof}

The ratio $\mu_F/\mu_G=\theta/(1-G)$ increases with $x$, from $\theta$ to infinity. Hence $\mu_F$ exceeds $\mu_G$ at every $x$ when $\theta\geq 1$. When $\theta<1$, it does so beyond the point where $G(x)=1-\theta$.

\begin{remark}
$F$ is an exponential distribution applied to the odds $G/(1-G)$. It is the T-X family of \citet{alzaatreh2013new} with an exponential generator $T$ and $W(G)=G/(1-G)$, and the Weibull-G family of \citet{bourguignon2014weibull} with the Weibull shape parameter fixed at one, which they call the exponential generator. \citet{bourguignon2014weibull} interpret the construction through the odds of death $G(x)/\{1-G(x)\}$ of a baseline lifetime: here, $X\leq x$ exactly when an exponential random variable with rate $\theta$ does not exceed these odds. They also note that it can be simulated as $G^{-1}\{T/(1+T)\}$, with $T$ drawn from the generator, which is Proposition~\ref{prop:family} combined with Proposition~\ref{prop:basic}(i).
\end{remark}

The Laguerre construction gives the following representation.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:series}
Let $w_k(\theta)=L_{k-1}(\theta)-L_k(\theta)$ for $k\geq1$. Then $w_k(\theta)=(\theta/k)\,L_{k-1}^{(1)}(\theta)$, and for every $x$ with $G(x)<1$,
\[
F(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} w_k(\theta)\, G(x)^k,
\qquad
f(x)=\theta\, g(x)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} L_k^{(1)}(\theta)\, G(x)^k.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:family} and Equation~\eqref{family}, with $G=G(x)$,
\[
1-F(x)=(1-G)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}L_n(\theta)G^n=L_0(\theta)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\{L_k(\theta)-L_{k-1}(\theta)\}G^k.
\]
Since $L_0=1$, this gives $F(x)=\sum_{k\geq1}w_k(\theta)G^k$. The series for $f$ follows from $f(x)=g(x)\,h\{G(x)\}$ (proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:family}) and Proposition~\ref{prop:basic}(iv). Both are power series in $G$ with radius of convergence one, so the series for $F$ can be differentiated term by term. This gives $f(x)=g(x)\sum_{k\geq1}k\,w_k(\theta)G^{k-1}$, and comparing coefficients with the series for $f$ gives $k\,w_k(\theta)=\theta L_{k-1}^{(1)}(\theta)$.
\end{proof}

Since $G^k$ is the distribution function of the maximum of $k$ independent copies from $G$, Proposition~\ref{prop:series} writes $F$ as a linear combination of distributions of maxima. Some weights $w_k(\theta)$ can be negative, so $F$ is not in general a mixture. Expectations under $F$ can still be computed term by term from those of the maxima. For example, $\E[X^r]=\sum_k w_k(\theta)\,\E[X_{k:k}^r]$, where $X_{k:k}$ is the maximum of $k$ independent copies from $G$, whenever the series converges. \citet[Section~4]{bourguignon2014weibull} expand the density of the Weibull-G family as a double series of densities of powers of $G$, obtained from the exponential series and the binomial series. With Weibull shape one, collecting the terms with the same power of $G$ turns the inner sum into a Laguerre polynomial, and the double series becomes the single series of Proposition~\ref{prop:series}. Proposition~\ref{prop:series} explains the role of the Laguerre polynomials in the family, but the computations in the rest of the paper do not need it. For the LW and GLW models, the stochastic representation of Proposition~\ref{prop:repr} gives the moments and the actuarial functions more directly.

\subsection{Exponential baseline}

If $G(x)=1-\e^{-\lambda x}$, then $G/(1-G)=\e^{\lambda x}-1$, and
\[
F(x)=1-\exp\{-\theta(\e^{\lambda x}-1)\},\qquad \mu_F(x)=\theta\lambda\,\e^{\lambda x},\qquad x>0.
\]
$X$ therefore follows the Gompertz law with initial level $\theta\lambda$ and rate $\lambda$.

\subsection{Weibull baseline}

If $G(x)=1-\exp(-ax^b)$, with $a,b>0$, then $G/(1-G)=\e^{ax^b}-1$, and
\begin{equation}\label{L-weibull}
F(x)=1- \exp\left\{-\theta\left(\e^{ax^b}-1\right)\right\},
\qquad
\mu_F(x)=\theta\,ab\,x^{b-1}\,\e^{ax^b}, \qquad x>0.
\end{equation}
We call this the Laguerre--Weibull (LW) distribution, and write $\Lambda_F(x)=\theta(\e^{ax^b}-1)$ for its cumulative hazard. With $\theta=\lambda\alpha$, $a=\alpha^{-\beta}$ and $b=\beta$, it is the Weibull extension of \citet{xie2002modified}, whose distribution function is $1-\exp[\lambda\alpha\{1-\e^{(x/\alpha)^\beta}\}]$. The model of \citet{chen2000new} is the case $a=1$.

\subsection{Shape of the LW hazard}

The parameter $\theta$ multiplies the hazard at every age. It sets the level of mortality and leaves its age pattern unchanged. The parameters $a$ and $b$ set the age pattern, and $a^{-1/b}$ acts as a scale for age, since $ax^b=(x/a^{-1/b})^b$. From
\[
\frac{\dd}{\dd x}\log\mu_F(x)=\frac{b-1}{x}+ab\,x^{b-1},
\]
the hazard is increasing when $b\geq1$. When $b=1$ it is the Gompertz hazard $\theta a\,\e^{ax}$, which grows at the constant relative rate $a$. When $b>1$ the relative rate of increase grows without bound at older ages, and mortality eventually rises faster than exponentially. When $0<b<1$ the hazard is bathtub-shaped. It decreases from infinity at $x=0$ to a minimum at $x^\ast=\{(1-b)/(ab)\}^{1/b}$ and increases afterwards \citep{xie2002modified}.

Figure~\ref{fig:lw} shows the density, survival function and hazard function of the LW distribution for four parameter sets, with the hazard on a log scale. The set with $b<1$ has a bathtub-shaped hazard. The set with $b=1$ has a Gompertz hazard, which is a straight line on the log scale. The two sets with $b>1$ have hazards that rise faster than exponentially.

\section{The gamma--Laguerre--Weibull model}\label{sec:glw}
%  ------------------------------------------------------------

The LW hazard describes a homogeneous population. At old ages, the selective survival of the more robust makes the population hazard rise more slowly than individual hazards, so a mortality model needs to allow for unobserved heterogeneity. To this end, we multiply the LW hazard by an unobserved frailty $Z$, so that $\mu(x\mid Z=z)=z\,\mu_F(x)$, where $Z$ follows a gamma distribution with $\E[Z]=1$ and $\Var[Z]=\sigma^2$ \citep{vaupel1979impact}. Integrating over $Z$ gives the population survival function $S(x)=\{1+\sigma^2 \Lambda_F(x)\}^{-1/\sigma^2}$, where $\Lambda_F(x)=\theta(\e^{ax^b}-1)$ is the cumulative LW hazard. Throughout, we write $\rho=\sigma^2\theta$.

\subsection{Basic functions}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:glw}
Under the gamma--Laguerre--Weibull (GLW) model, the survival function and the hazard function are, for $x>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{L-weibull-frailty}
S(x)=\left\{1+\rho\left(\e^{ax^b}-1\right)\right\}^{-1/\sigma^2}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\bar{\mu}(x) = \frac{\theta\, a b\, x^{b - 1}\, \e^{a x^{b}}}{1 + \rho \left(\e^{a x^{b}} - 1\right)}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Given $Z=z$, the survival function is $\exp\{-z\Lambda_F(x)\}$, so $S(x)=\E[\exp\{-Z\Lambda_F(x)\}]$. This is the Laplace transform of $Z$ evaluated at $\Lambda_F(x)$. For a gamma distribution with mean 1 and variance $\sigma^2$, $\E[\e^{-sZ}]=(1+\sigma^2s)^{-1/\sigma^2}$, which gives $S(x)$. The hazard is $\bar\mu(x)=-\dd\log S(x)/\dd x$.
\end{proof}

The GLW model reduces to the LW distribution as $\sigma^2\to0$, and to the gamma-Gompertz model when $b=1$. Every result below is stated for the GLW model, and the corresponding LW result follows by letting $\sigma^2\to0$. Where the limit has a simpler form, we state it separately. We write GLW$(\theta,a,b,\sigma^2)$ and LW$(\theta,a,b)$ when the parameter values need to be shown.

\subsection{Shape of the GLW hazard}

From Equation~\eqref{L-weibull-frailty},
\[
\frac{\dd}{\dd x}\log\bar\mu(x)=\frac{b-1}{x}+ab\,x^{b-1}\,\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho\left(\e^{ax^b}-1\right)}.
\]
Compared with the LW hazard, frailty multiplies the second term by a factor that equals $1-\rho$ at $x=0$ and decreases to zero as $x\to\infty$. We consider $\rho<1$, the relevant case for human mortality. As $x\to0$, $\bar\mu(x)$ behaves like the LW hazard $\theta ab\,x^{b-1}$, so it tends to zero when $b>1$, to $\theta a$ when $b=1$, and to infinity when $0<b<1$. When $\sigma^2>0$ and $x\to\infty$, $\bar\mu(x)$ behaves like $ab\,x^{b-1}/\sigma^2$. When $b=1$ the hazard increases and levels off at $a/\sigma^2$, as in the gamma-Gompertz model. When $b>1$ it increases without bound, but its relative rate of increase tends to zero, so it grows only polynomially at older ages. When $0<b<1$ it decreases near $x=0$ and declines to zero at older ages, possibly after rising over an intermediate range. When $\sigma^2=0$, $\bar\mu$ is the LW hazard, which grows without bound for every $b>0$ because of the factor $\e^{ax^b}$. Because $b$ is estimated mainly from ages with many deaths, the hazard implied at the oldest ages is an extrapolation.

Figure~\ref{fig:glw} shows the density, survival function and hazard function of the GLW model for four parameter sets, with the hazard on a log scale. The two sets with $b=1$ level off at $a/\sigma^2$, which equals 2 for $\sigma^2=0.5$ and 0.5 for $\sigma^2=2$. The set with $b<1$ declines towards zero after its initial decrease. The set with $b>1$ keeps increasing, although slowly, since $\bar\mu(x)$ behaves like $ab\,x^{b-1}/\sigma^2$.

\subsection{Stochastic representation}

The remaining subsections characterise the distribution of a GLW lifetime $X$. Most of the results follow from a representation of the transformed lifetime $U=aX^b$, given in the next proposition.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:repr}
Let $X$ follow the GLW$(\theta,a,b,\sigma^2)$ model, let $U=aX^b$ and let $V=\rho\,(\e^{U}-1)$.
\begin{enumerate}\renewcommand{\labelenumi}{(\roman{enumi})}
\item $\Pr(V>v)=(1+v)^{-1/\sigma^2}$ for $v>0$, so that $V$ has a Pareto type II (Lomax) distribution with shape $1/\sigma^2$ and unit scale.
\item $U=\log(1+V/\rho)$ and $X=(U/a)^{1/b}$, and $U$ follows the GLW$(\theta,1,1,\sigma^2)$ model, that is, a gamma-Gompertz model with unit rate.
\item Under the LW$(\theta,a,b)$ model, $\theta(\e^{U}-1)=\Exp$ is standard exponential, so that $U=\log(1+\Exp/\theta)$ and $X=[a^{-1}\log\{1+\Exp/\theta\}]^{1/b}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) For $v>0$, $V>v$ if and only if $X>x_v$, where $x_v=\{a^{-1}\log(1+v/\rho)\}^{1/b}$. Since $1+\rho(\e^{ax_v^b}-1)=1+v$, Proposition~\ref{prop:glw} gives $\Pr(V>v)=S(x_v)=(1+v)^{-1/\sigma^2}$.

(ii) Solving $V=\rho(\e^{U}-1)$ for $U$ gives the first identity. For $u>0$, $\Pr(U>u)=\Pr\{V>\rho(\e^u-1)\}=\{1+\rho(\e^u-1)\}^{-1/\sigma^2}$, which is the survival function of Proposition~\ref{prop:glw} with $a=b=1$.

(iii) The LW distribution is the Laguerre-G distribution with Weibull baseline $G(x)=1-\exp(-ax^b)$, so $X=G^{-1}(Y)$ with $Y\sim\Lag(\theta)$, and $G(X)/\{1-G(X)\}=\e^{U}-1$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:basic}(i), $Y/(1-Y)=\Exp/\theta$.
\end{proof}

Part (ii) reduces every expectation of a function of $X$ to an expectation over a gamma-Gompertz variable with unit rate, and the future lifetime at age $x$ will turn out to have the same structure (Proposition~\ref{prop:future}). Part (iii), with $\theta$ replaced by $z\theta$ for an individual with frailty $z$, is how the lifetimes are generated in the simulation study of Section~\ref{sec:simulation}. Part (i) also gives a direct way to simulate the GLW model without simulating the frailties: draw $V$ from the Lomax distribution and set $X=\{a^{-1}\log(1+V/\rho)\}^{1/b}$.

\subsection{Moments}

The moments involve two special functions. The first is the generalised Hurwitz--Lerch zeta function of \citet{goyal1997generalized},
\[
\Phi^\ast_\omega(z,s,v)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\omega)_n}{n!}\,\frac{z^n}{(n+v)^s}
=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_0^\infty t^{s-1}\e^{-vt}\left(1-z\e^{-t}\right)^{-\omega}\dd t,
\]
where $(\omega)_n=\Gamma(\omega+n)/\Gamma(\omega)$. The series converges for $|z|<1$, and the integral defines $\Phi^\ast_\omega$ for every $z<1$ when $s,v>0$. The second is the generalised integro-exponential function of \citet{milgram1985generalized},
\[
E_s^j(z)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(j+1)}\int_1^\infty(\log t)^j\,t^{-s}\,\e^{-zt}\,\dd t,
\]
which reduces to the exponential integral $E_1(z)=\int_z^\infty t^{-1}\e^{-t}\,\dd t$ when $s=1$ and $j=0$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:moments-glw}
Under the GLW$(\theta,a,b,\sigma^2)$ model, for $r>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:glw-moments}
\E[X^r]=\frac{\Gamma(r/b+1)}{\sigma^2\,a^{r/b}\,\rho^{1/\sigma^2}}\;\Phi^\ast_{1+1/\sigma^2}\!\left(1-\frac1\rho,\;\frac rb+1,\;\frac1{\sigma^2}\right).
\end{equation}
Under the LW$(\theta,a,b)$ model,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lw-moments}
\E[X^r]=\theta\,\e^{\theta}\,\Gamma(r/b+1)\,a^{-r/b}\,E_0^{r/b}(\theta).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:repr}(ii), $X^r=(U/a)^{r/b}$, and $U$ has survival function $\{1+\rho(\e^u-1)\}^{-1/\sigma^2}$ and density $f_U(u)=\sigma^{-2}\rho\,\e^{u}\{1+\rho(\e^u-1)\}^{-1/\sigma^2-1}$, $u>0$. Write $1+\rho(\e^u-1)=\rho\,\e^u(1-\xi\e^{-u})$ with $\xi=1-1/\rho<1$. Then $f_U(u)=\sigma^{-2}\rho^{-1/\sigma^2}\e^{-u/\sigma^2}(1-\xi\e^{-u})^{-1/\sigma^2-1}$, and
\[
\E[X^r]=\frac{\rho^{-1/\sigma^2}}{\sigma^2 a^{r/b}}\int_0^\infty u^{r/b}\,\e^{-u/\sigma^2}\left(1-\xi\e^{-u}\right)^{-(1+1/\sigma^2)}\dd u.
\]
The integral representation of $\Phi^\ast_\omega$ with $\omega=1+1/\sigma^2$, $s=r/b+1$ and $v=1/\sigma^2$ shows that the integral equals $\Gamma(r/b+1)\,\Phi^\ast_{1+1/\sigma^2}(\xi,r/b+1,1/\sigma^2)$, which gives Equation~\eqref{eq:glw-moments}.

Under the LW model, $U=\log(1+\Exp/\theta)$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:repr}(iii), so
\[
\E[X^r]=a^{-r/b}\int_0^\infty\{\log(1+y/\theta)\}^{r/b}\e^{-y}\,\dd y=a^{-r/b}\,\theta\,\e^{\theta}\int_1^\infty(\log t)^{r/b}\e^{-\theta t}\,\dd t,
\]
using $t=1+y/\theta$. By the definition of $E_s^j$, the last integral is $\Gamma(r/b+1)E_0^{r/b}(\theta)$, which gives Equation~\eqref{eq:lw-moments}.
\end{proof}

The moments depend on $r$ and $b$ only through $r/b$, and on $a$ only through the factor $a^{-r/b}$. For $b=1$ and $r=1$, Equation~\eqref{eq:lw-moments} gives the mean of the Gompertz distribution, $\e^\theta E_1(\theta)/a$, since $E_0^1(\theta)=E_1(\theta)/\theta$ after integrating by parts. With $a=1$ it gives the moments of the distribution of \citet{chen2000new}, which \citet{pogany2017extension} obtained in terms of an extension of $E_s^j$. For the Gompertz and Gompertz--Makeham cases, \citet{lenart2014moments} and \citet{castellares2022gompertz} used the same function.

The mean of $U=aX^b$ deserves a separate statement. When $b=1$, it is $a$ times the remaining life expectancy, and Section~\ref{sec:actuarial} uses it at every age. Since $\E[U]=a\,\E[X^b]$, it is given by Equation~\eqref{eq:glw-moments} with $r=b$, and in this case the Hurwitz--Lerch function reduces to a Gauss hypergeometric function. The reduction rests on the following lemma.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:log}
Let $\Pr(V>v)=(1+v)^{-1/\sigma^2}$, $v>0$, and let $\lambda>0$. Then
\[
\E[\log(1+\lambda V)]=\lambda\sigma^2\;{}_2F_1\!\left(1,1;1+\frac1{\sigma^2};1-\lambda\right)=\sigma^2\;{}_2F_1\!\left(1,\frac1{\sigma^2};1+\frac1{\sigma^2};1-\frac1\lambda\right),
\]
where ${}_2F_1$ is the Gauss hypergeometric function. Both expressions hold for every $\lambda>0$, with ${}_2F_1(p,q;c;w)$ defined for $w<1$ by Euler's integral below; the hypergeometric series converges for $0<\lambda<2$ in the first expression and for $\lambda>1/2$ in the second.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Integrating by parts, $\E[\log(1+\lambda V)]=\int_0^\infty\lambda(1+\lambda v)^{-1}\Pr(V>v)\,\dd v$. The substitution $v=t/(1-t)$ turns this into
\[
\lambda\int_0^1(1-t)^{1/\sigma^2-1}\{1-(1-\lambda)t\}^{-1}\,\dd t=\lambda\sigma^2\;{}_2F_1\!\left(1,1;1+\frac1{\sigma^2};1-\lambda\right),
\]
by Euler's integral representation ${}_2F_1(p,q;c;w)=\{\Gamma(c)/[\Gamma(q)\Gamma(c-q)]\}\int_0^1t^{q-1}(1-t)^{c-q-1}(1-wt)^{-p}\dd t$, $c>q>0$ \citep[Chapter~9]{lebedev1965special}. The second form follows from Pfaff's transformation ${}_2F_1(p,q;c;w)=(1-w)^{-p}{}_2F_1\{p,c-q;c;w/(w-1)\}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:EU}
Under the GLW$(\theta,a,b,\sigma^2)$ model, the mean of $U=aX^b$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:EU}
\E[U]=\frac{1}{\sigma^2\,\rho^{1/\sigma^2}}\;\Phi^\ast_{1+1/\sigma^2}\!\left(1-\frac1\rho,\;2,\;\frac1{\sigma^2}\right)=\sigma^2\;{}_2F_1\!\left(1,\frac1{\sigma^2};1+\frac1{\sigma^2};1-\rho\right).
\end{equation}
Under the LW$(\theta,a,b)$ model, $\E[U]=\e^\theta E_1(\theta)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The first expression is $a$ times Equation~\eqref{eq:glw-moments} with $r=b$; equivalently, it is Equation~\eqref{eq:glw-moments} with $a=1$ and $r=b$, since then $X^r=U$. It is also Equation~\eqref{eq:glw-moments} with $a=b=r=1$, because $U$ follows the GLW$(\theta,1,1,\sigma^2)$ model by Proposition~\ref{prop:repr}(ii). For the second expression, $U=\log(1+V/\rho)$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:repr}(ii), and Lemma~\ref{lem:log} with $\lambda=1/\rho$ gives $\E[U]$. Under the LW model, $U=\log(1+\Exp/\theta)$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:repr}(iii), and $\E[U]=\int_0^\infty(\theta+y)^{-1}\e^{-y}\,\dd y=\e^\theta E_1(\theta)$ after integrating by parts.
\end{proof}

The two expressions in Equation~\eqref{eq:EU} can also be matched directly, by an integration by parts and Pfaff's transformation (Appendix~\ref{app:EU}). Lemma~\ref{lem:log} also gives the Shannon entropy of the GLW model and the Kullback--Leibler divergence between two GLW models with common $a$ and $b$, which have explicit forms in terms of $\E[U]$ and ${}_2F_1$ (Appendix~\ref{app:entropy}).

\subsection{Generating functions}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:mgf}
Let $m_r=\E[X^r]$ be given by Proposition~\ref{prop:moments-glw}. Under the GLW model, the moment generating function, the characteristic function and the Laplace transform are
\[
M_X(t)=1+\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\frac{t^r}{r!}\,m_r,
\qquad
\varphi_X(t)=M_X(\mathrm{i}t),
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_X(s)=M_X(-s).
\]
The series converge for every real $t$ when $b>1$, and for $|t|<a/\sigma^2$ when $b=1$. Under the LW model, they converge for every real $t$ and every $b>0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $X\geq0$, monotone convergence gives
\[
\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{|t|^r}{r!}\,m_r=\E\left[\e^{|t|X}\right],
\]
with $m_0=1$. The series therefore converges absolutely, and equals $M_X(t)$, whenever $\E[\e^{|t|X}]<\infty$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:glw}, $S(x)\,\rho^{1/\sigma^2}\exp(ax^b/\sigma^2)\to1$ as $x\to\infty$. Hence $\E[\e^{tX}]=1+t\int_0^\infty\e^{tx}S(x)\,\dd x$ is finite for every $t$ when $b>1$, finite for $t<a/\sigma^2$ and infinite for $t\geq a/\sigma^2$ when $b=1$, and infinite for every $t>0$ when $b<1$. Under the LW model, $S(x)=\exp\{-\theta(\e^{ax^b}-1)\}$ decays faster than $\e^{-tx}$ for every $t$, so $\E[\e^{tX}]<\infty$ for every $t$ and every $b>0$. The expressions for $\varphi_X$ and $\mathcal{L}_X$ follow because $|\e^{\mathrm{i}tX}|=1$ and $\e^{-sX}\leq\e^{|s|X}$.
\end{proof}

When $b<1$, $M_X(t)$ is infinite for $t>0$ under the GLW model, because the right tail is heavier than exponential. The Laplace transform $\mathcal{L}_X(s)$, $s>0$, still exists, but it must be computed as $1-s\int_0^\infty\e^{-sx}S(x)\,\dd x$. In Section~\ref{sec:actuarial}, $\mathcal{L}_X(\delta)$ is the value of a whole life assurance at the starting age.

%  ------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Actuarial properties}\label{sec:actuarial}
%  ------------------------------------------------------------

We now use the results of Section~\ref{sec:glw} to derive the quantities used for pricing and reserving. We use the notation of \citet{dickson2019actuarial}. Age is measured from the starting age of the model, so that $x=0$ corresponds to the first age at which the model applies (age 50 in Section~\ref{sec:application}). Let $(x)$ denote a life aged $x$ and $T_x$ its future lifetime, so that $x+T_x$ is the age at death and $T_0=X$. We assume a constant force of interest $\delta$ per year.

\subsection{Future lifetime and life expectancy}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:future}
Under the GLW model, let $u_x=ax^b$ and
\[
\theta_x=\frac{\theta\,\e^{u_x}}{1+\rho\,(\e^{u_x}-1)}.
\]
Then the probability that $(x)$ survives $t$ more years is
\[
{}_tp_x=\frac{S(x+t)}{S(x)}=\left[1+\sigma^2\theta_x\left\{\e^{a(x+t)^b-u_x}-1\right\}\right]^{-1/\sigma^2},\qquad t\geq0.
\]
Equivalently, $U_x=a(x+T_x)^b-u_x$ has the distribution of $U=aX^b$ under the GLW$(\theta_x,a,b,\sigma^2)$ model, that is, the GLW$(\theta_x,1,1,\sigma^2)$ distribution of Proposition~\ref{prop:repr}(ii), and $T_x=\{(u_x+U_x)/a\}^{1/b}-x$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The proof follows the survivors. Given $Z=z$, the probability of surviving from age $x$ to age $x+t$ is $\exp\{-z\theta(\e^{a(x+t)^b}-\e^{u_x})\}$. Among the survivors to age $x$, the frailty is again gamma distributed, with the same shape parameter $1/\sigma^2$, mean $\bar z_x=1/\{1+\sigma^2\Lambda_F(x)\}$ and variance $\sigma^2\bar z_x^2$ \citep{vaupel1979impact}. Hence $Z=\bar z_x Z_x$ for the survivors, where $Z_x$ has mean one and variance $\sigma^2$. Since $\theta\bar z_x\e^{u_x}=\theta_x$, the conditional survival probability is $\exp\{-Z_x\theta_x(\e^{w}-1)\}$ with $w=a(x+t)^b-u_x$, and averaging over $Z_x$ gives ${}_tp_x=\{1+\sigma^2\theta_x(\e^{w}-1)\}^{-1/\sigma^2}$, as in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:glw}. Since $T_x>t$ if and only if $U_x>w$, this is also $\Pr(U_x>w)$, which is the survival function of $U$ in Proposition~\ref{prop:repr}(ii) with $\theta_x$ in place of $\theta$. The same formula follows directly from $S(x+t)/S(x)$ and the identity $1+\rho(\e^{u_x+w}-1)=\{1+\rho(\e^{u_x}-1)\}\{1+\sigma^2\theta_x(\e^{w}-1)\}$.
\end{proof}

The parameter $\theta_x=\theta\,\e^{u_x}\bar z_x$ is the level parameter of the survivors to age $x$. The factor $\e^{u_x}$ is the ageing of every individual since the starting age, and the factor $\bar z_x$ is the selection that has removed the frailest. On the time scale $u=ax^b$, a life aged $x$ is therefore a new life at the starting age, with mortality level $\theta_x$ and the same frailty variance: $a(x+T_x)^b-ax^b$ has the distribution that $aX^b$ has at the starting age when $\theta$ is replaced by $\theta_x$. The parameter $\theta_x$ equals $\theta$ at $x=0$ and tends to $1/\sigma^2$ as $x\to\infty$ when $\sigma^2>0$; it increases with $x$ when $\rho<1$ and decreases when $\rho>1$. Proposition~\ref{prop:future} is the result used in the application: ${}_tp_x$ is explicit, and every actuarial value below is an integral of ${}_tp_x$. When $b=1$, $U_x=aT_x$, so $T_x$ follows the gamma-Gompertz model with parameter $\theta_x$, and the closed-form expressions of \citet{castellares2024gamma} apply at every age.

The remaining life expectancy of $(x)$ is
\[
\mathring{e}_x=\E[T_x]=\int_0^\infty{}_tp_x\,\dd t.
\]
Closed forms are available in two cases, given in the next corollary. When $b\neq1$ and $x>0$, $T_x=\{(u_x+U_x)/a\}^{1/b}-x$ is a nonlinear function of $U_x$, and we do not have a closed form for $\mathring{e}_x$. It is then a one-dimensional integral of ${}_tp_x$, which is available in closed form.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:ex}
Let $\rho_x=\sigma^2\theta_x$, with $\theta_x$ as in Proposition~\ref{prop:future}.
\begin{enumerate}\renewcommand{\labelenumi}{(\roman{enumi})}
\item At the starting age, $\mathring{e}_0=\E[X]$ is given by Equation~\eqref{eq:glw-moments} with $r=1$.
\item When $b=1$, for every $x\geq0$, the remaining life expectancy and the whole life annuity at force of interest $\delta\geq0$ (Section~\ref{sec:annuities}) are
\begin{align*}
\mathring{e}_x&=\frac{\sigma^2}{a}\;{}_2F_1\!\left(1,\frac1{\sigma^2};1+\frac1{\sigma^2};1-\rho_x\right),\\
\bar{a}_x&=\frac{1}{a/\sigma^2+\delta}\;{}_2F_1\!\left(\frac1{\sigma^2},1;1+\frac1{\sigma^2}+\frac\delta a;1-\rho_x\right).
\end{align*}
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Part (i) holds because $T_0=X$. For part (ii), when $b=1$, $U_x=aT_x$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:future}, so $T_x$ has the GLW$(\theta_x,a,1,\sigma^2)$ distribution and $\mathring{e}_x=\E[U_x]/a$. Corollary~\ref{cor:EU} with $\rho_x$ in place of $\rho$ gives $\mathring{e}_x$. For $\bar{a}_x$, substitute $v=\e^{-at}$ in $\int_0^\infty\e^{-\delta t}\{1-\rho_x+\rho_x\e^{at}\}^{-1/\sigma^2}\dd t$, which gives $a^{-1}\rho_x^{-1/\sigma^2}\int_0^1v^{1/\sigma^2+\delta/a-1}\{1-(1-1/\rho_x)v\}^{-1/\sigma^2}\dd v$, and apply Euler's integral and Pfaff's transformation as in Lemma~\ref{lem:log}.
\end{proof}

Part (ii) is the gamma-Gompertz case of the closed forms of \citet[Proposition~5 and Corollary~5]{castellares2024gamma}, written in the present parametrisation; the life expectancy is the annuity with $\delta=0$. The form of $\mathring{e}_x$ also follows from Equation~\eqref{eq:glw-moments} with $\theta_x$ in place of $\theta$, by the reduction of the Hurwitz--Lerch function given after Corollary~\ref{cor:EU}.

\subsection{Life annuities and assurances}\label{sec:annuities}

The expected present value (EPV) of a whole life annuity paid continuously at rate 1 per year to $(x)$, and the EPV of a whole life assurance with unit benefit paid immediately on the death of $(x)$, are
\[
\bar{a}_x=\int_0^\infty\e^{-\delta t}\,{}_tp_x\,\dd t,
\qquad
\bar{A}_x=\E\left[\e^{-\delta T_x}\right]=1-\delta\,\bar{a}_x.
\]
The variance of the present value of the assurance is ${}^2\bar{A}_x-\bar{A}_x^2$, where ${}^2\bar{A}_x$ is $\bar{A}_x$ evaluated at force of interest $2\delta$. For an $n$-year term,
\[
\bar{a}_{x:\overline{n}|}=\int_0^n\e^{-\delta t}\,{}_tp_x\,\dd t,
\qquad
\bar{A}_{x:\overline{n}|}=1-\delta\,\bar{a}_{x:\overline{n}|},
\qquad
\bar{A}^{\,1}_{x:\overline{n}|}=\bar{A}_{x:\overline{n}|}-\e^{-\delta n}\,{}_np_x,
\]
where $\bar{A}_{x:\overline{n}|}$ is the $n$-year endowment assurance and $\bar{A}^{\,1}_{x:\overline{n}|}$ the $n$-year term assurance. The annual premium for a whole life assurance with unit benefit is $\bar{P}(\bar{A}_x)=\bar{A}_x/\bar{a}_x$, and the prospective reserve at duration $t$ is ${}_t\bar{V}(\bar{A}_x)=\bar{A}_{x+t}-\bar{P}(\bar{A}_x)\,\bar{a}_{x+t}$.

Since $\bar{A}_x=\E[\e^{-\delta T_x}]$ is the Laplace transform of $T_x$ evaluated at $\delta$, the cases of Corollary~\ref{cor:ex} again give explicit expressions. At the starting age, $\bar{A}_0=\mathcal{L}_X(\delta)$ and $\bar{a}_0=\{1-\mathcal{L}_X(\delta)\}/\delta$, with $\mathcal{L}_X$ as in Proposition~\ref{prop:mgf}; the series converges for $b>1$, and for $b=1$ when $\delta<a/\sigma^2$. When $b=1$, $\bar{a}_x$ is given at every age by Corollary~\ref{cor:ex}(ii), and $\bar{A}_x=1-\delta\bar{a}_x$. The temporary annuity then follows from $\bar{a}_{x:\overline{n}|}=\bar{a}_x-\e^{-\delta n}\,{}_np_x\,\bar{a}_{x+n}$, and the endowment and term assurances from the relations above. When $b\neq1$ and $x>0$, all the EPVs above are one-dimensional integrals of ${}_tp_x$, which we evaluate numerically.

\subsection{Joint-life and last-survivor statuses}

Consider two independent lives, both aged $x$, whose mortality follows GLW models with common parameters $a$ and $b$. The joint-life status $(xx)$ fails at the first death, and the last-survivor status $(\overline{xx})$ fails at the second death \citep[Chapter~7]{neill1977life}.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:joint}
Let the two lives follow GLW models with parameters $(\theta_1,a,b,\sigma_1^2)$ and $(\theta_2,a,b,\sigma_2^2)$, with $\sigma_1^2\theta_1=\sigma_2^2\theta_2$. Then the joint-life status has the survival probabilities of a single life aged $x$ under the GLW model with parameters
\[
\left(\theta_1+\theta_2,\;a,\;b,\;\frac{\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2}\right).
\]
Two lives with identical parameters give the GLW model with parameters $(2\theta,a,b,\sigma^2/2)$. Under the LW model, the joint-life status follows the LW model with parameters $(\theta_1+\theta_2,a,b)$, with no restriction on $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $\rho=\sigma_1^2\theta_1=\sigma_2^2\theta_2$. Since the lives are independent, ${}_tp_{xx}={}_tp_x^{(1)}\,{}_tp_x^{(2)}=S^\ast(x+t)/S^\ast(x)$, where
\[
S^\ast(y)=S_1(y)S_2(y)=\left\{1+\rho\left(\e^{ay^b}-1\right)\right\}^{-(1/\sigma_1^2+1/\sigma_2^2)}.
\]
This is the GLW survival function of Proposition~\ref{prop:glw} with $1/\sigma^2=1/\sigma_1^2+1/\sigma_2^2$ and $\sigma^2\theta=\rho$, that is, with $\theta=\rho/\sigma^2=\theta_1+\theta_2$. Under the LW model, $S_1(y)S_2(y)=\exp\{-(\theta_1+\theta_2)(\e^{ay^b}-1)\}$ for any $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$.
\end{proof}

All results of the previous subsections therefore apply to the joint-life status. For example, $\bar{a}_{xx}$ and $\bar{A}_{xx}=1-\delta\bar{a}_{xx}$ are single-life values computed with the parameters of Proposition~\ref{prop:joint}. Write $\bar{a}^{(j)}_x$ for the single-life annuity of life $j$. The last-survivor annuity is $\bar{a}_{\overline{xx}}=\bar{a}^{(1)}_x+\bar{a}^{(2)}_x-\bar{a}_{xx}$, and the reversionary annuity payable to life 2 after the death of life 1 is $\bar{a}^{(2)}_x-\bar{a}_{xx}$ \citep[Chapter~13]{jordan1967life}. For lives of different ages, the joint-life status under the Gompertz law ($b=1$ and $\sigma^2\to0$) can be replaced by a single life at an equivalent age, as shown by \citet[Section~9.6]{jordan1967life} and \citet[Section~7.2]{neill1977life}. Under the Gompertz--Makeham law with a common rate, \citet[Lemma~3]{castellares2022gompertz} show in the same way that the joint-life status of lives of different ages is again Gompertz--Makeham, and for the gamma-Gompertz--Makeham model \citet[Proposition~8]{castellares2024gamma} express $\bar{a}_{xy}$ through Appell's hypergeometric function of two variables. We have not found a reduction of this kind for lives of different ages under the GLW model with $b\neq1$.

\section{Statistical inference}\label{sec:inference}
%  ------------------------------------------------------------

The actuarial quantities of Section~\ref{sec:actuarial} depend on parameters that, in practice, are estimated from death counts and exposures. This section gives the likelihood, its score and information, and the test of the gamma-Gompertz restriction.

\subsection{Poisson likelihood}

The data consist of death counts and exposures to risk by single year of age. Let $x_0$ be the starting age of the analysis, and let $D_x$ and $E^c_x$ be the number of deaths and the central exposure to risk, in person-years, in the age interval $[x_0+x,\,x_0+x+1)$, $x=0,\ldots,m$. As in Section~\ref{sec:actuarial}, age in the model is measured from $x_0$. Write $\bm{\psi} = (\theta, a, b, \sigma^2)^\top$ for the parameter vector and $\mu_x(\bm{\psi})=\bar{\mu}(y_x)$ for the GLW hazard of Proposition~\ref{prop:glw} at the midpoint $y_x=x+1/2$ of the interval. Since $y_x>0$, the hazard is defined also when $b<1$.

Following \citet{brillinger1986biometrics}, we assume that, given the exposures, the death counts are independent with $D_x\sim\text{Poisson}\{E^c_x\,\mu_x(\bm{\psi})\}$. The log-likelihood is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:poisson_log_likelihood}
\ell(\bm{\psi}) = \sum_{x} \left\{ D_x \log \mu_x(\bm{\psi}) - E^c_x\, \mu_x(\bm{\psi}) \right\},
\end{equation}
where the sum is over $x=0,\ldots,m$ and terms that do not depend on $\bm{\psi}$ are omitted. The maximum likelihood estimate $\hat{\bm{\psi}}$ has no closed form. We maximise Equation~\eqref{eq:poisson_log_likelihood} under the constraints $\theta,a,b>0$ and $\sigma^2\geq0$, with the routine {\tt nlminb} of {\sf R} \citep{R2024} and the analytical score of Section~\ref{sec:score}. We use several starting values and restart from the best solution until the log-likelihood no longer increases. Since the LW model corresponds to $\sigma^2=0$, it belongs to the parameter space and the maximum can lie on its boundary. We call an estimate a boundary estimate when $\hat\sigma^2<10^{-8}$, and we then set $\hat\sigma^2=0$.

\subsection{Score functions and Fisher information}\label{sec:score}

The score for a component $\psi_j$ of $\bm{\psi}$ is
\[
\frac{\partial \ell(\bm{\psi})}{\partial \psi_j}=\sum_{x}r_x(\bm{\psi})\,\frac{\partial\mu_x(\bm{\psi})}{\partial\psi_j},
\qquad
r_x(\bm{\psi})=\frac{D_x}{\mu_x(\bm{\psi})}-E^c_x.
\]
Let $\eta_x=\e^{ay_x^b}$ and $\kappa_x=1+\theta\sigma^2(\eta_x-1)$. The derivatives of the GLW hazard are
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial \mu_x}{\partial \theta} &= \frac{a b\, y_x^{b-1}\eta_x}{\kappa_x^2}, \\
\frac{\partial \mu_x}{\partial a} &= \frac{\theta b\, y_x^{b-1}\eta_x}{\kappa_x} + \frac{\theta a b\, y_x^{2b - 1}\eta_x \left(1-\theta \sigma^2\right)}{\kappa_x^2}, \\
\frac{\partial \mu_x}{\partial b} &= \frac{\theta a\, y_x^{b-1} \eta_x \left(1 + b \log y_x\right)}{\kappa_x} + \frac{\theta a^2 b\, y_x^{2b - 1}\eta_x \left(1-\theta \sigma^2\right) \log y_x}{\kappa_x^2}, \\
\frac{\partial \mu_x}{\partial \sigma^2} &= -\frac{\theta^2 a b\, y_x^{b-1}\eta_x \left(\eta_x-1\right)}{\kappa_x^2}.
\end{align*}
Since $\E[D_x]=E^c_x\mu_x(\bm{\psi})$, the expected Fisher information is
\[
\bm{K}(\bm{\psi})=\sum_{x}\frac{E^c_x}{\mu_x(\bm{\psi})}\,\frac{\partial\mu_x(\bm{\psi})}{\partial\bm{\psi}}\,\frac{\partial\mu_x(\bm{\psi})}{\partial\bm{\psi}^\top}.
\]
The observed information $-\partial^2\ell(\bm{\psi})/\partial\bm{\psi}\,\partial\bm{\psi}^\top$, evaluated at $\hat{\bm{\psi}}$, can be used in place of $\bm{K}(\hat{\bm{\psi}})$.

Approximate $100(1 - \zeta)\%$ confidence intervals are $\hat{\psi}_j \pm z_{\zeta/2}\, \operatorname{se}(\hat{\psi}_j)$. Here $z_{\zeta/2} = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \zeta/2)$, and $\operatorname{se}(\hat{\psi}_j)$ is the square root of the $j$th diagonal element of $\bm{K}(\hat{\bm{\psi}})^{-1}$. These intervals assume that $\bm{\psi}$ is an interior point. When $\hat\sigma^2=0$, the interval for $\sigma^2$ is not valid, and we report it with its lower limit set to zero only as a rough indication of precision.

\subsection{Testing the gamma-Gompertz model}

The gamma-Gompertz model is the GLW model with $b=1$. To test $H_0\colon b=1$ against $H_1\colon b\neq1$, we use the likelihood-ratio statistic
\[
\mathrm{LR}=2\left\{\ell(\hat{\bm{\psi}})-\ell(\tilde{\bm{\psi}})\right\},
\]
where $\tilde{\bm{\psi}}=(\tilde\theta,\tilde a,1,\tilde\sigma^2)^\top$ maximises Equation~\eqref{eq:poisson_log_likelihood} under $H_0$. Since $b=1$ is an interior point of the parameter space, LR is asymptotically $\chi^2_1$ under $H_0$, and $H_0$ is rejected at level $\zeta$ when LR exceeds the $(1-\zeta)$ quantile of the $\chi^2_1$ distribution. This holds when the true $\sigma^2$ is positive. If $\sigma^2=0$, the nuisance parameter lies on the boundary of the parameter space, and the null distribution of LR can differ from $\chi^2_1$ \citep{self1987asymptotic}. For such cases we calibrate the test by a parametric bootstrap. We generate $B$ sets of death counts $D_x^\ast\sim\text{Poisson}\{E^c_x\,\mu_x(\tilde{\bm{\psi}})\}$ from the fitted gamma-Gompertz model, refit both models to each set to obtain $\mathrm{LR}^\ast_1,\ldots,\mathrm{LR}^\ast_B$, and compute the $p$-value $\{1+\#(\mathrm{LR}^\ast_r\geq\mathrm{LR})\}/(B+1)$ \citep[Chapter~4]{davison1997bootstrap}.

\section{Simulation study}\label{sec:simulation}
%  ------------------------------------------------------------

Before turning to data, we assess the maximum likelihood estimators and the likelihood-ratio test of Section~\ref{sec:inference} by Monte Carlo simulation. Each replication generates a closed cohort of $n$ individuals from the GLW model in three steps. First, the frailties $Z_1,\ldots,Z_n$ are drawn from a gamma distribution with mean 1 and variance $\sigma^2$. Second, given $Z_i=z_i$, the lifetime of individual $i$ follows the LW$(z_i\theta,a,b)$ distribution, with survival function $\exp\{-z_i\theta(\e^{ax^b}-1)\}$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:repr}(iii), we set
\[
X_i=\left[\frac1a\log\left\{1+\frac{\Exp_i}{z_i\theta}\right\}\right]^{1/b},
\]
where $\Exp_1,\ldots,\Exp_n$ are independent standard exponential random variables, generated as $\Exp_i=-\log(\text{uniform})$. This is inversion of the LW distribution function. Third, the lifetimes are grouped into the intervals $[x,x+1)$, $x=0,1,\ldots$, up to the interval of the last death, so that $x_0=0$. For each interval, $D_x$ is the number of deaths and $E^c_x$ is the number of person-years lived. The data are thus generated at the individual level, and the Poisson assumption enters only through the likelihood in Equation~\eqref{eq:poisson_log_likelihood}.

The parameter values follow a $2^4$ factorial design, with $\theta_1=0.02$, $\theta_2=0.04$, $a_1=0.075$, $a_2=0.10$, $b_1=1$, $b_2=1.1$, $\sigma^2_1=0.05$ and $\sigma^2_2=0.1$. The eight scenarios with $b=1$ are gamma-Gompertz models and give the size of the likelihood-ratio test of $H_0\colon b=1$; the other eight give its power. We consider cohorts of $n=20{,}000$ and $n=50{,}000$ individuals and run 1{,}000 replications for each of the 32 combinations. All computations were carried out in {\sf R} \citep{R2024}. The log-likelihood is maximised as described in Section~\ref{sec:inference}. We report the absolute bias, the root mean squared error (RMSE), the empirical standard deviation (SD) of the estimates, the average asymptotic standard error (ASE) obtained from $\bm{K}(\hat{\bm{\psi}})^{-1}$, the coverage probability (CP) of the 95\% Wald confidence intervals, and the rejection rate (RR) of the likelihood-ratio test at the 5\% level. The optimisation converged in all replications.

Table~\ref{tab:sim} shows that the estimates are close to the true values in all scenarios. The absolute bias is small relative to the RMSE. For $b$, it is at most 0.014 when $n=20{,}000$ and at most 0.010 when $n=50{,}000$, while the RMSE ranges from 0.086 to 0.110 and from 0.053 to 0.069. On average, the RMSE is 1.6 times larger for the smaller cohort, close to the factor $\sqrt{2.5}\approx1.58$ expected from the $\sqrt{n}$ rate of convergence.

The table also compares the ASE with the empirical SD. The ASE tends to be smaller than the SD. The ratio SD/ASE ranges from 0.99 to 1.42 when $n=20{,}000$ and from 1.01 to 1.16 when $n=50{,}000$. Consequently, the Wald intervals exhibit undercoverage at these cohort sizes. The CP ranges from 0.858 to 0.969, with mean 0.909, when $n=20{,}000$, and from 0.886 to 0.952, with mean 0.922, when $n=50{,}000$. Coverage improves as $n$ increases, although it remains below the nominal level in several scenarios. In the smaller cohort, $\sigma^2$ was estimated at the boundary $\hat\sigma^2=0$ in 303 of the 16{,}000 replications, against 20 in the larger cohort, which may also contribute to the lower coverage at $n=20{,}000$.

The likelihood-ratio test holds its nominal level. Under $H_0$, the rejection rate ranges from 0.042 to 0.063 when $n=20{,}000$ and from 0.044 to 0.060 when $n=50{,}000$, so the $\chi^2_1$ approximation is adequate. For $b=1.1$, the power ranges from 0.207 to 0.259 and from 0.412 to 0.465. A departure of 0.1 in $b$ is therefore hard to detect in cohorts of these sizes, but the power grows with $n$.

\section{Application to human mortality data}\label{sec:application}
%  ------------------------------------------------------------

We fit the GLW model to female death counts and exposures from the Human Mortality Database \citep{hmd} for Denmark, France, England and Wales, Italy, Sweden and the United States, for every calendar year from 1950 to 2020. We use the single ages 50 to 109, so that $x_0=50$ in Section~\ref{sec:inference}, and $x=0$ in the model of Section~\ref{sec:glw} corresponds to age 50. The fitted model therefore describes the population alive at age 50, and $\sigma^2$ measures heterogeneity among women alive at that age, not in the population at birth. Near age 50, the fitted hazard tends to zero when $\hat b>1$ and to infinity when $\hat b<1$ (Section~\ref{sec:glw}). The model is fitted as described in Section~\ref{sec:inference}, and the optimisation converged for all 426 populations. As in \citet{castellares2022gompertz, castellares2024gamma}, we fit the model separately to single calendar years by Poisson maximum likelihood and compare the fitted remaining life expectancies with those of the HMD life tables at three ages.

\subsection{Parameter estimates}

Figures~\ref{fig:est_b} and~\ref{fig:est_sigma2} show the estimates of the shape parameter $b$ and the frailty variance $\sigma^2$ with their 95\% Wald confidence intervals, and Figures~\ref{fig:est_theta} and~\ref{fig:est_a} in Appendix~\ref{app:application} show those of $\theta$ and $a$. The intervals are narrowest for the United States and widest for Denmark, in line with the size of the populations.

The six countries follow a common pattern. In 1950, $\hat b$ lies between 0.93 and 0.98 and $\hat a$ between 0.11 and 0.15, so the hazard after age 50 is close to the gamma-Gompertz form. By 2020, $\hat b$ has increased to between 1.23 and 1.37 and $\hat a$ has fallen to between 0.02 and 0.04 (Figure~\ref{fig:est_a}). The rise in $\hat b$ is gradual and occurs in all six countries, so the departure from the gamma-Gompertz form is a systematic feature of these data rather than of a few years or populations. The level parameter $\hat\theta$ decreased in France, England and Wales, Italy and Sweden, increased slightly in Denmark and changed little in the United States (Figure~\ref{fig:est_theta}). The frailty variance is a boundary estimate, $\hat\sigma^2=0$, in 111 of the 426 fits: 49 for the United States, 42 for Denmark, 19 for France and one for England and Wales. For these fits the interval for $\sigma^2$ in Figure~\ref{fig:est_sigma2} is not a valid confidence interval (Section~\ref{sec:score}), and the fitted model is the homogeneous LW distribution, so any gain over the gamma-Gompertz model comes from the shape parameter $b$ and not from heterogeneity.

The likelihood-ratio test of Section~\ref{sec:inference} rejects $H_0\colon b=1$ at the 5\% level in 74\% of the fits. The rejection rate is 22\% for the years 1950--1969, 84\% for 1970--1989 and 100\% for 1990--2020. From 1990 on, 159 of the 186 fits have $\sigma^2$ estimated at zero under the GLW model, the gamma-Gompertz model or both, so the $\chi^2_1$ reference distribution may not apply. For each of these fits we ran the parametric bootstrap of Section~\ref{sec:inference} with $B=200$. In every case the observed LR, which is at least 9.5, exceeded all 200 bootstrap values, so the bootstrap $p$-value is $1/201\approx0.005$, the smallest attainable. The 95\% quantiles of the bootstrap distributions lie between 2.7 and 5.5, close to the $\chi^2_1$ value of 3.84. The rejection of the gamma-Gompertz model after 1990 therefore does not depend on the $\chi^2_1$ approximation. In these data, the additional shape parameter captures a change in old-age mortality that has become more pronounced over time.

\subsection{Comparison with other mortality laws}

To see whether the extra flexibility of the GLW model improves on established mortality laws, and not only on its gamma-Gompertz submodel, we fit the Gompertz law $\mu(x)=\alpha\e^{\beta x}$ and the Kannisto law $\mu(x)=\alpha\e^{\beta x}/(1+\alpha\e^{\beta x})$ \citep{thatcher1998force}, which has the logistic form of Perks' law \citep[Table~5.1]{macdonald2018modelling}, to the same 426 populations, ages and likelihood. Like the GLW and gamma-Gompertz models, both describe senescent mortality only, so we do not include laws with an age-independent Makeham term. For each population-year and each model $m$, we compute $\mathrm{AIC}_m=2k_m-2\ell_m$, where $k_m$ is the number of parameters and $\ell_m$ the maximised Poisson log-likelihood, and the AIC difference $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}_m=\mathrm{AIC}_m-\min_{m'}\mathrm{AIC}_{m'}$, where the minimum is over the four models fitted to the same population-year. The model with the lowest AIC has $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}_m=0$, and the other models have positive values that measure how much worse they fit after the penalty for the number of parameters. As a rule of thumb for actuarial work, a difference of four or more AIC units is regarded as significant \citep[Section~6.4.1]{macdonald2018modelling}. We also compute the error of the remaining life expectancy at ages 50, 65 and 80. Since the models are fitted and evaluated on the same population-years, these errors measure their in-sample reproduction of the life-table quantities rather than out-of-sample forecasting performance. Table~\ref{tab:benchmark} summarises the results. In Appendix~\ref{app:application}, Figure~\ref{fig:bench_daic} shows $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}$ by country and year, Figure~\ref{fig:bench_hazards} the fitted hazards in 2020, and Figures~\ref{fig:bench_ex50} to~\ref{fig:bench_ex80} the errors in the remaining life expectancy.

The GLW model has the lowest AIC in 78\% of the population-years. Its median $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}$ is therefore zero, since $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=0$ in more than half of the population-years, and the median of the other models is the typical loss of fit relative to the best model. We report medians rather than means because $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}$ grows with the number of deaths, so the mean is driven by the largest populations. For example, for the United States in 2020 the gamma-Gompertz model has $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=4{,}999$. The mean $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}$ is 0.3 for the GLW model and 310, 352 and 1{,}508 for the gamma-Gompertz, Gompertz and Kannisto models. The advantage of the GLW model is concentrated in recent decades. The GLW model has the lowest AIC in 32.5\% of the population-years in 1950--1969, when $\hat b$ is close to 1 and the gamma-Gompertz model is preferred in 62.5\% of them, in 88\% in 1970--1989 and in all population-years from 1990. The ranking does not depend on the penalty for the extra parameter: with BIC instead of AIC, the GLW model is preferred in 72.5\% of the population-years. The gain in life expectancy is smaller than the gain in likelihood, but it is consistent at older ages. The mean absolute error of $\mathring{e}_{65}$ is 0.145 years for the GLW model, against 0.159 to 0.214 years for the other three laws, and that of $\mathring{e}_{80}$ is 0.088 years, against 0.091 to 0.109 years. At age 50, the Gompertz, gamma-Gompertz and GLW models are equally accurate, with mean absolute errors of about 0.03 years, while the Kannisto law has twice that error. The GLW model does not remove the remaining errors: all four laws underestimate $\mathring{e}_{65}$ in recent years (Figure~\ref{fig:bench_ex65}).

\section{Concluding remarks}\label{sec:conclusion}
%  ------------------------------------------------------------

Annuity values depend on the shape of the force of mortality at ages where data are sparse, and a parametric law fixes much of that shape in advance. The gamma-Gompertz model, Beard's law in actuarial terms, imposes a plateau whenever the frailty variance is positive. We studied the GLW model, which adds gamma frailty to the LW distribution, itself the Weibull extension of \citet{xie2002modified}. Its derivation from the generating function of the Laguerre polynomials gives a series representation in terms of distributions of maxima, and, for positive frailty variance, a single shape parameter, $b$, determines whether the population hazard grows without bound, levels off or declines at old ages.

The survival and hazard functions are in closed form, and the moments, the generating functions, the Shannon entropy and the Kullback--Leibler divergence have explicit representations in terms of special functions and series. At every age $x$, $a(x+T_x)^b-ax^b$ has the distribution of $aX^b$ with an age-dependent $\theta_x$, so the future lifetime stays in the gamma-Gompertz family when $b=1$. The joint-life status of two lives of the same age is again a GLW model when $\sigma_1^2\theta_1=\sigma_2^2\theta_2$. Life annuities and assurances are explicit at every age when $b=1$ and at the starting age when $b>1$, and one-dimensional integrals otherwise. In the simulation study, the Poisson maximum likelihood estimators have small bias and the likelihood-ratio test of $H_0\colon b=1$ keeps its nominal level. The Wald intervals exhibit undercoverage at the cohort sizes considered, although coverage improves as the cohort grows.

In the application to female mortality after age 50, $\hat b$ increased from just below 1 in 1950 to between 1.23 and 1.37 in 2020 in all six countries, and the gamma-Gompertz model is rejected in every year from 1990, also when the test is calibrated by the bootstrap. The GLW model does not dominate the established laws everywhere. In the 1950s and 1960s, when $\hat b$ is close to 1, the gamma-Gompertz model is usually preferred, and at age 50 the Gompertz law reproduces the remaining life expectancy as well as the GLW model. The GLW model is usually preferred from 1970 to 1989 and is preferred in every population-year from 1990, and it gives modest but consistent gains in the accuracy of the remaining life expectancy at ages 65 and 80.

When $\sigma^2>0$, $\bar\mu(x)$ behaves like $ab\,x^{b-1}/\sigma^2$ at older ages, so for $b>1$ the GLW hazard grows polynomially without bound and cannot level off. When $\hat\sigma^2=0$, as in 111 of the fits, the hazard is the LW hazard, which grows faster because of the factor $\e^{ax^b}$. In both cases the hazard implied beyond the range of the data should be used with care, in particular for annuities sold at advanced ages. The choice also has a prudential side. A hazard that levels off gives higher survival probabilities at the oldest ages than one that keeps rising, and \citet{gbari2017extreme} regard a constant ultimate force of mortality as a conservative assumption for annuity business. When $\hat b>1$, the GLW model replaces that assumption with the shape estimated from the data, and an actuary who prefers a prudent basis can still close the table with a plateau beyond the observed ages. A Makeham term \citep{makeham1860law} would add background mortality to the model. Its survival factor $\e^{-ct}$ combines with the discount factor, so annuity values would follow from those of Section~\ref{sec:actuarial} at force of interest $\delta+c$; we did not pursue this extension because background mortality is small after age 50 and $c$ would be weakly identified.

Within the data analysed, the additional shape parameter captures a systematic change in old-age mortality that the gamma-Gompertz model cannot represent. The model allows a frailty interpretation and keeps the gamma-Gompertz model as a testable special case and the actuarial quantities in tractable form. The data, however, do not always support heterogeneity: in 111 of the 426 fits the frailty variance is estimated at zero, the fitted model is the homogeneous LW distribution, and the improvement in fit comes from $b$ alone.

\section{Further distributional properties}\label{app:dist}

This appendix collects distributional results that the main text does not need for the actuarial calculations: the moments of the Laguerre distribution of Section~\ref{sec:laguerre}, a direct proof that the two expressions of Corollary~\ref{cor:EU} agree, and the Shannon entropy and Kullback--Leibler divergence of the GLW model.

\subsection{Moments of the Laguerre distribution}\label{app:laguerre-moments}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:moments}
Let $Y\sim\Lag(\theta)$, let $E_1$ be the exponential integral, and let $q(\theta)=\theta\,\e^{\theta}E_1(\theta)$. For $n\geq 1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:moments}
\E[Y^n]= n \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{L_k(\theta)}{(k+n)(k+n+1)}.
\end{equation}
The first two moments and the variance also have the closed forms
\[
\E[Y]=1-q(\theta), \qquad \E[Y^2]=1+\theta-(2+\theta)\,q(\theta), \qquad \Var[Y]=\theta\{1-q(\theta)\}-q(\theta)^2.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For a random variable on $(0,1)$, $\E[Y^n]=\int_0^1 n y^{n-1}\Pr(Y>y)\,\dd y$. With $\Pr(Y>y)=(1-y)\sum_{k\geq0}L_k(\theta)y^k$ from Equation~\eqref{family},
\[
\E[Y^n]=n\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L_k(\theta)\int_0^1 y^{n+k-1}(1-y)\,\dd y=n\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{L_k(\theta)}{(k+n)(k+n+1)},
\]
which is Equation~\eqref{eq:moments}. Integrating term by term is justified because $|L_k(\theta)|\leq\e^{\theta/2}$ and $\sum_k\{(k+n)(k+n+1)\}^{-1}<\infty$.

For the closed forms, write $Y=1-(1+T)^{-1}$ with $T=\Exp/\theta$, which has density $\theta\e^{-\theta t}$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:basic}(i)). By the definition of $E_1$,
\[
\E\left[(1+T)^{-1}\right]=\theta\int_0^\infty\frac{\e^{-\theta t}}{1+t}\,\dd t=\theta\,\e^{\theta}E_1(\theta)=q(\theta).
\]
Integrating by parts,
\[
\E\left[(1+T)^{-2}\right]=\theta\int_0^\infty\frac{\e^{-\theta t}}{(1+t)^2}\,\dd t=\theta\left\{1-\theta\int_0^\infty\frac{\e^{-\theta t}}{1+t}\,\dd t\right\}=\theta\{1-q(\theta)\}.
\]
Hence $\E[Y]=1-q(\theta)$ and $\E[Y^2]=1-2\E[(1+T)^{-1}]+\E[(1+T)^{-2}]=1+\theta-(2+\theta)q(\theta)$. The variance is $\E[Y^2]-\E[Y]^2$.
\end{proof}

Equating the series in Equation~\eqref{eq:moments} with $n=1$ with the closed form of $\E[Y]$ gives a series for the exponential integral,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:E1}
E_1(\theta)=\frac{\e^{-\theta}}{\theta}\left\{\frac12 - \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{L_k(\theta)}{(k+1)(k+2)}\right\}, \qquad \theta>0.
\end{equation}
The series converges, since its terms are bounded by $\e^{\theta/2}/\{(k+1)(k+2)\}$, but slowly, because $L_k(\theta)$ oscillates in $k$ with an amplitude that decays only like $k^{-1/4}$ \citep[Section~8.22]{szeg1939orthogonal}. Equation~\eqref{eq:E1} is therefore of interest as an identity between the Laguerre polynomials and the exponential integral, and not as a way to compute $E_1$.

\subsection{The two expressions for the mean of $U$}\label{app:EU}

The two expressions for $\E[U]$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:EU} come from different routes: the first from the moments of Proposition~\ref{prop:moments-glw}, the second from Lemma~\ref{lem:log}. They can also be matched directly. Write $v=1/\sigma^2$ and $\xi=1-1/\rho$. Integrating by parts in the integral that defines $\Phi^\ast_{v+1}(\xi,2,v)$, with $\dd\{\e^{-vt}(1-\xi\e^{-t})^{-v}\}/\dd t=-v\,\e^{-vt}(1-\xi\e^{-t})^{-v-1}$, gives $\Phi^\ast_{v+1}(\xi,2,v)=v^{-1}\Phi^\ast_{v}(\xi,1,v)$. Since $1/(n+v)=(v)_n/\{v\,(v+1)_n\}$, the series of $\Phi^\ast_{v}(\xi,1,v)$ is $v^{-1}{}_2F_1(v,v;v+1;\xi)$, so the first expression equals $\sigma^2\rho^{-1/\sigma^2}{}_2F_1(v,v;v+1;\xi)$. Pfaff's transformation, with $1-\xi=1/\rho$ and $\xi/(\xi-1)=1-\rho$, turns this into the second expression. The hypergeometric form is simpler to evaluate, and it is the one used in Corollary~\ref{cor:ex}. The same reduction holds under the LW model, where $\theta\e^\theta E_0^1(\theta)=\e^\theta E_1(\theta)$.

\subsection{Shannon entropy and Kullback--Leibler divergence}\label{app:entropy}

The entropy and the Kullback--Leibler divergence complete the statistical description of the GLW model. Both follow from the representation of Proposition~\ref{prop:repr} and from Lemma~\ref{lem:log}.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:entropy}
Under the GLW model, the Shannon entropy $\mathcal{H}(X)=-\E[\log f(X)]$ is
\[
\mathcal{H}(X)=1+\sigma^2-\log(\theta ab)-\E[U]-\frac{b-1}{b}\left(\E[\log U]-\log a\right),
\]
with $\E[U]$ given by Corollary~\ref{cor:EU} and $\E[\log U]=\partial\,\E[U^s]/\partial s$ at $s=0$, where $\E[U^s]$ is Equation~\eqref{eq:glw-moments} with $a=b=1$ and $r=s$, because $U$ follows the GLW$(\theta,1,1,\sigma^2)$ model (Proposition~\ref{prop:repr}(ii)). Under the LW model, $\mathcal{H}(X)=1-\log(\theta ab)-\e^\theta E_1(\theta)-(1-1/b)(\E[\log U]-\log a)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:glw}, $f(x)=\bar\mu(x)S(x)$, so
\[
\log f(x)=\log(\theta ab)+(b-1)\log x+ax^b-\left(1+\frac1{\sigma^2}\right)\log\{1+\rho(\e^{ax^b}-1)\}.
\]
Take expectations, with $U=aX^b$ and $V=\rho(\e^U-1)$ as in Proposition~\ref{prop:repr}. The second term gives $(b-1)\E[\log X]$ with $\E[\log X]=(\E[\log U]-\log a)/b$, and the third gives $\E[U]$. In the last term, $1+\rho(\e^{aX^b}-1)=1+V$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:repr}(i), $\Pr\{\log(1+V)>w\}=\e^{-w/\sigma^2}$, so $\log(1+V)$ is exponential with mean $\sigma^2$, and the last term contributes $(1+\sigma^{-2})\sigma^2=1+\sigma^2$. Under the LW model, $\log f(x)=\log(\theta ab)+(b-1)\log x+ax^b-\theta(\e^{ax^b}-1)$, and $\theta(\e^{U}-1)$ is standard exponential by Proposition~\ref{prop:repr}(iii), so the last term contributes 1; $\E[U]=\e^\theta E_1(\theta)$ by Corollary~\ref{cor:EU}.
\end{proof}

When $b=1$, the last term vanishes and $\mathcal{H}(X)$ depends only on $\E[U]$, which is explicit by Corollary~\ref{cor:EU}. The Kullback--Leibler divergence has an explicit form when the two models share the parameters $a$ and $b$, which is the relevant case when comparing the level of mortality or the degree of heterogeneity.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:kl}
Let $f_1$ and $f_2$ be the densities of the GLW$(\theta_1,a,b,\sigma_1^2)$ and GLW$(\theta_2,a,b,\sigma_2^2)$ models, and let $\rho_j=\sigma_j^2\theta_j$, $j=1,2$. The Kullback--Leibler divergence of $f_2$ from $f_1$,
\[
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(f_1\,\|\,f_2)=\int_0^\infty f_1(x)\log\frac{f_1(x)}{f_2(x)}\,\dd x,
\]
is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kl}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(f_1\,\|\,f_2)=\log\frac{\theta_1}{\theta_2}-\left(1+\sigma_1^2\right)+\left(1+\frac1{\sigma_2^2}\right)\sigma_1^2\;{}_2F_1\!\left(1,\frac1{\sigma_1^2};1+\frac1{\sigma_1^2};1-\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2}\right).
\end{equation}
The hypergeometric term can also be written in the first form of Lemma~\ref{lem:log} with $\lambda=\rho_2/\rho_1$. Under the LW model, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(f_1\,\|\,f_2)=\log(\theta_1/\theta_2)-1+\theta_2/\theta_1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $X$ have density $f_1$, and let $U=aX^b$ and $V_1=\rho_1(\e^U-1)$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:glw}, the terms in $\log x$ and $ax^b$ cancel in $\log\{f_1(x)/f_2(x)\}$, and
\[
\log\frac{f_1(X)}{f_2(X)}=\log\frac{\theta_1}{\theta_2}-\left(1+\frac{1}{\sigma_1^2}\right)\log\{1+\rho_1(\e^U-1)\}+\left(1+\frac{1}{\sigma_2^2}\right)\log\{1+\rho_2(\e^U-1)\}.
\]
Here $1+\rho_1(\e^U-1)=1+V_1$ and $1+\rho_2(\e^U-1)=1+(\rho_2/\rho_1)V_1$, where $V_1$ has the Lomax distribution of Proposition~\ref{prop:repr}(i) with $\sigma^2=\sigma_1^2$. As in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:entropy}, $\E[\log(1+V_1)]=\sigma_1^2$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:log} with $\lambda=\rho_2/\rho_1$ gives $\E[\log\{1+(\rho_2/\rho_1)V_1\}]$. Under the LW model, $\log\{f_1(X)/f_2(X)\}=\log(\theta_1/\theta_2)-(\theta_1-\theta_2)(\e^U-1)$, and $\E[\e^U-1]=1/\theta_1$ because $\theta_1(\e^U-1)$ is standard exponential under $f_1$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:repr}(iii)).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Estimates of $\theta$ and $a$}

Figures~\ref{fig:est_theta} and~\ref{fig:est_a} complete Figures~\ref{fig:est_b} and~\ref{fig:est_sigma2} with the estimates of the level parameter $\theta$ and the age-scale parameter $a$.

\begin{figure}[htb!]

\subsection{Model fit over time and fitted hazards}

Figure~\ref{fig:bench_daic} shows $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}$ for the four mortality laws of Section~\ref{sec:application} by country and year, and Figure~\ref{fig:bench_hazards} shows the observed and fitted hazards in 2020. In Figure~\ref{fig:bench_daic}, the model with the lowest AIC in a given year lies on the horizontal axis ($\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=0$), and the height of each other line is its loss of fit relative to that model. The GLW line lies on the axis in most years from 1970 onwards because the GLW model is then the best of the four, while in the 1950s and 1960s the gamma-Gompertz line is often on the axis and the GLW line slightly above it. Since the GLW model contains the gamma-Gompertz model, its maximised log-likelihood is never lower, so its $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}$ relative to the gamma-Gompertz model is at most 2, the penalty for the extra parameter.

In each year, the model with the lowest AIC has $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}=0$; larger values mean a worse fit relative to that model. The vertical axis is on a $\log(1+\Delta\mathrm{AIC})$ scale.}
